02The Return

The hardest ramp anyone will ever ask him to do, he already did

Most résumés hide a career gap. This one should lead with it — because of what happened on the other side. Jason stepped out of technology in 2003 to raise his family, working part-time EMS for nearly seven of those years. In 2019 he re-certified. Within roughly fifteen months of that decision, he was the top-rated sales engineer in the Americas for his organization.

Seventeen years out. Nine months to number one.

Every hiring manager's real question about a domain shift is the same: how long until this person is productive? Jason has a measured answer, produced under the worst possible starting conditions — no recent industry exposure, a brand-new employer, a 100% channel-driven model where he did not own the customer relationship, and a pandemic that made every engagement virtual.

And it was not a matter of picking up where he left off

Cloud computing did not exist in 2003. Neither did XDR, CSPM, DSPM, EVPN/VXLAN, AI-driven network operations, or attack surface risk management. Coming back was not reactivating an old skill set — it was learning technologies that had been invented during his absence, and then reaching expert level in them. He learned cloud from scratch in the 2019 certificate, and by January 2023 held an AWS Solutions Architect credential.

The engineering judgment is the point, not the output

He diagnosed a metric rather than picking one. Jaccard similarity was silently killing deduplication because it penalizes length mismatch — a 160-word article and a 70-word write-up of the same story scored 0.24. Switching to the overlap coefficient nearly doubled the separation between true duplicates and false pairs (0.185 vs 0.094), and the threshold was set from that measurement rather than by feel.

And he found out the hard way that prompt-level honesty instructions are not a control. Run against an empty vendor pack, the model invented an "80% alert volume reduction" and "$X per year" in savings instead of reporting the gap it had been told to report. The fix was a completeness banner computed in code, not requested from the model. That is AI governance learned by watching it fail — the applied version of the Wharton course above.

Local model by default (Ollama) because discovery notes carry customer names, budgets, and competitive intelligence — with a hosted API as the alternative and, deliberately, no offline fallback: a demo plan generated without a model is a template with the customer's name pasted in, which is worse than an honest failure. The outstanding work is vendor pack content — the schema, tooling, and completeness reporting are built; the curated demo flows and competitive positioning are not yet written.

Why this is the right artifact for him to have built

It is not a generic AI demo. It automates the exact bottleneck Jason personally lived through at Nugenesis, Juniper, and Trend Micro — turning discovery into a demo that lands with two audiences who need entirely different things. The dual talk track is the whole insight, and it is the same translation skill that runs through every section above.

His own assessment of where the value sits is the tell: the generation layer is a commodity — any curious SE can paste notes into a chatbot and get a competent summary. What no individual SE will do is curate and maintain, across a team, the demo flows that actually win, honest competitive positioning, and POC success criteria. That is organizational knowledge, it decays without an owner, and it walks out the door when a senior SE leaves. The vendor packs are the moat, and they are the outstanding work.

Stated limitations, from the project's own README: the vendor packs are empty, so output quality is bounded by that; the model invents specifics when the pack is thin; a local model is slow (~4 tokens/second on CPU); there is no UI; and there is no evaluation yet of whether the generated plans are actually any good — judging that needs real discovery notes and a written pack first.

08Cumulative Value

The case, in one place

Hiring Jason is not a bet on what he knows today. It is a bet on how fast he becomes the person your customers trust — and that is the one thing his career has measured, repeatedly, under increasingly hard conditions.

He has done this four times. Internetworking at Cisco and AT&T. Enterprise data archiving and compliance at Nugenesis. Enterprise networking, EVPN/VXLAN, and SRX security at Juniper. He has seen both sides of the sale. He knows exactly what happens after the signature, which is why his POCs are honest ones.

He teaches, at every single stop. At Nugenesis he enabled the lab users who inherited the product. At Juniper he built and delivered the training for the entire virtual sales organization across wireless, firewalls, routing, and switching — and ran a go-to-market entirely through VARs, distributors, and partner SEs. At Trend Micro he enabled new customers on the platform post-sale so they became genuinely efficient with it. Nobody asked him to make enablement part of the job; it has been part of the job for thirty years.

And he translates under pressure. That thread runs from an ambulance in Wake County, through a global Cisco WAN NOC at AT&T, through EVPN architecture reviews, to executive value reports that turn raw security telemetry into a remediation punch list a CISO can act on Monday morning.

The AI pivot is not a career change. It is the fifth run of a play he has already won with — and as of October 2025, it is underway.
